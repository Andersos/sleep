The story of Sleep · Chapter one

The Second Time Around

A night elf hunter named Diod, a launch-day queue, and how a guild called Sleep assembled itself around a spreadsheet. Autumn 2019 to spring 2020.

Diod

The story starts fifteen years before the first log. In 2005, a teenager in Nittedal played the original World of Warcraft as Diod, a night elf hunter on Frostwhisper. It was an era of carrying computers to each other's houses and losing whole afternoons in Stranglethorn Vale. Nobody understood gear. That was never the point. When Classic launched on 27 August 2019, Artimis came back to recreate that feeling as another night elf hunter, and half of Nittedal came with him. The first archived message of the whole story is Fnats, level 10, asking from the launch-day queue: "Er du i queue?"

The queue was not a metaphor. On 27 August 2019, Artimis and Fnats were each stuck in it for hours on the new realm, Razorgore, trading messages while the login screen refused to move. It was a smaller, more solitary echo of the LAN nights fifteen years earlier, minus the shared living room. Fnats would later misremember the realm's name as "Razorgrove". Close enough. Neither man was chasing a raid schedule that week. They were chasing the version of the game where nobody understood gear yet, and for a few days of queueing and slow leveling, Classic delivered exactly that.

Sleep

The guild he found, probably through a recruitment shout in Ironforge, was called Sleep, and it committed to the joke. Its ranks ran Awake, Light Sleeper, Deep Sleeper, REM Sleeper, Dreamer. Its founder was the priest Dejvid, and his pitch was a helpful community for newcomers and veterans alike. Artimis joined to collect hunter tier pieces. Within a month he was in the officer meetings, running the hunter channel and the tranquilizing-shot rotation. He also volunteered for the signup sheets, which turned out to decide the rest of his Classic career. The guild's practical first Molten Core raid, on 2 October 2019, survives as a screenshot of a nearly full room gathering before Magmadar, guild tags still mixed, a team assembling itself in real time.

The job that came with those signup sheets showed itself first in small arbitrations, not spreadsheets. By 10 November, Knedlica had already won a better hunter gun while Ravna and Nereb were still carrying weaker bows. Artimis asked whether the two of them could take the next Ancient Petrified Leaves first, and Knedlica agreed, then supplied his own weapon-speed theorycraft to help the case. Artimis finished his own Rhok'delar around 11 November. He had joined for the bow. He was learning the guild's loot policy and its raid mechanics in the same conversation, one polite hunter-channel negotiation at a time.

Then, on 11 November 2019, a hunter named Zados sent his class leader a polite suggestion. Nobody from the guild was uploading to Warcraft Logs. Perhaps the officers should look into it? The next morning, after helping with the setup, he signed off: "Sorry to bother you." Artimis turned on advanced combat logging that day. Five hundred and five raids are searchable on this site because of that message.

The Nittedal circle becomes visible in those first logs rather than in one surviving recruitment post. The 13 November full clear includes Artimis, Fnats, Deliz, Ragie and Exclus. It was Ragie's own first logged raid, three weeks after Exclus, already at home in the guild, vouched for him in the mage channel as "a really good tank." After New Year, Pnats and Galidia appear too. These are first surviving raid dates, not necessarily the nights they joined. What had started as Artimis returning to an old game was becoming a home crew inside a much larger guild.

Onyxia and Ragnaros

Progress came faster than the surviving logs suggest. On 13 October Sleep recorded its first documented Onyxia kill and finished the Molten Core run at eight bosses out of ten. A month later, the first complete report preserved by Zados's suggestion shows all ten Molten Core bosses dying without a wipe. A surviving recording is titled Sleep's first Ragnaros kill, and Artimis remembers 13 November as probably that night. The video itself does not settle the date. What began as a mixed group in the Magmadar corridor had become a full-clear raid in six weeks.

Sleep's first Ragnaros kill

By spring, Onyxia was familiar enough that a failure could become a souvenir rather than a disaster. On 11 March 2020, Ragie disconnected while tanking her, and the rest of Sleep improvised long enough to survive the pull. Artimis's report from that night identifies the raid. The video went up the next day, and what it preserves is the room recovering together when its tank simply stopped moving.

Onyxia disconnect, 11 March

Onyxia settled into double groups and Molten Core became routine. Then the loot gave the farm nights a story of their own. On 29 December another Ragnaros kill produced the Choker of the Fire Lord, one of the rarest drops in the raid and best-in-slot for every caster and every healer in it. The young loot rules gave damage casters priority, because the neck was a bigger upgrade for them than for the healers. The healers noticed. Nobody said much that night. The argument the necklace started would outlive the guild that looted it. It resurfaced five weeks later as an officer-agenda line, "Priest Rag Choker," logged on both 3 and 5 February, with Artimis proposing a transparent roll queue after noticing that Deliz seemed unhappy. On the night of the 5th he wrote to Deliz directly. He pointed to a Blackwing Lair neck that healers would get first, and he spelled out the queue: four mage raiders and one warlock needed the Choker, and after them the healers could roll on it. The fix arrived a beat too late. That same night Deliz, a healer from the Nittedal circle and one of the guild's own, quit the game entirely over how healers were treated. He was the story's first fairness casualty. His question, what a raid owes the people who keep it alive but never top a meter, came back in every crisis the guild ever had.

The friendship outlasted the exit. DMs between Deliz and Artimis kept running for years afterward. Game recommendations, life updates, the occasional piece of guild news passed back in from the outside. An ordinary thread running quietly underneath the guild's harder history.

The paperwork catches up

By January 2020 the paperwork was behind the reality. Dejvid had taken on more responsibility at work and was playing less. Artimis, already running the hunter channel, the tranq rotation and the signup sheets, was the one members asked when something needed deciding. On 29 January Dejvid gave him admin rights on the guild Discord. The next day Thepaladin opened a tank-planning message with "since you took Dejvids place now" and briefed him on the missing main tank, a member addressing Artimis as the leader before the leadership itself formally changed hands. Around the same time Artimis asked Dejvid whether the guild bank could fund Ragie's gear and a run of Onyxia Scale Cloaks. Dejvid's reply was that he was "super happy" someone was taking care of the guild. Admin rights only went so far, though. On 1 February Artimis found he could not demote the vanished main tank, because only the server owner could do that. So he asked Dejvid to transfer the ownership itself. Dejvid's answer was one word: "Done." The title had simply followed the work that was already being done.

His first fortnight in the job set the tone for the year. He accidentally recruited his most loyal tank by mailing a cloak to a stranger he had mistaken for someone else, then inviting him to Blackwing Lair anyway. The stranger was Glory. The same month, Glorya, another player from the Nittedal circle, was recruited into the healer gap Deliz left behind. People mixed up the two similar names all year.

The tank goes missing

The absent main tank Thepaladin had briefed Artimis about was Kugel, the warrior the guild's first raids had been built around, and the man who had personally recruited Thepaladin into Sleep. "He brought me to guild," Thepaladin wrote, "hope he will come back, first person i got to know." By late January Kugel had gone twenty-three days without logging in. Artimis warned that if he stayed silent through the following week they would have to consider him a "gonner" and find a new main tank. Nobody in the surviving record ever explains where he went. In March, after two months offline, he was quietly removed from the roster.

The vacancy handed the tank mantle to Ragie, Exclus's referral from three weeks before Kugel's last login. It also taught the guild a lesson it wrote into its loot rules. Kugel had been given an early Binding of the Windseeker on the obvious logic that a legendary component belongs with the main tank. When he vanished, the Binding went with him. From then on, legendary pieces went to the players the guild trusted to keep showing up, not just to whoever held the right role at the time. The same promotion planted the guild's longest-running loot argument. Glory's complaint that "Ragie gets anything he wants" already dates to that March, and it put the mild-mannered tank leader repeatedly opposite the guild's most soft-spoken warrior for the rest of the year, on a question neither man ever fully won: what a main tank deserves first claim on. Zhèna, another Nittedal warrior, became a full member on 16 February and started filling the same gap on short notice. He was one of several players who would each, in turn, carry the front line through an undergeared week over the year that followed, because the guild had learned not to build itself around one person.

Ten days in Blackwing Lair

Blackwing Lair opened on 13 February and Sleep entered on day one. The first surviving progression log begins three days later. Razorgore fell on the second pull and Vaelastrasz on the fifth. Broodlord died on the first. Then Firemaw stopped the raid eleven times. They left with the drake at fifteen percent and their first real wall of the new tier.

The wall did not break by itself. A healer from another guild, impressed enough by what he had seen to reach out, sent Ragie a tank-switch video and offered to coach the positioning. Between raid nights the guild pooled the ore for several Dark Iron pieces so its tanks could survive the drake's flame. Even the Onyxia-head world buff was scheduled by the hour, until one player killed the NPC early and forced the reset himself. None of it was elegant. All of it was the same instinct that had produced the attendance workbook: solve the problem directly in front of you, then write down how.

On 19 February Firemaw died on the first attempt. Sleep cleared the remaining drakes and finished the night with Chromaggus, leaving only the throne room. Four days later, Nefarian died on the second pull. Blackwing Lair was eight bosses long and ten days old. For a guild built around helping newcomers, clearing it that quickly was the first proof that being welcoming and being capable were not opposites.

Sleep's first Firemaw kill

Some of that competence came from outside the guild. Starting in December 2019, Sleep and a guild called Ninjas had quietly begun trading favours: Sleep crafted Onyxia Scale Cloaks for Ninjas, Artimis referred a mage their way, and by February the two guilds were lending each other spare priests, paladins and warriors for Blackwing Lair without shorting either roster's own loot. A Ninjas officer, Snjór, shared detailed Nefarian tactics before Sleep's own kill and called the arrangement, simply, "the alliance." Eight months later, that same relationship would decide what the guild became next.

The machine

What made Sleep unusual wasn't its kill times. It was its paperwork. The attendance workbook. Ragie's gear-weight formulas, sketched in January DMs: parses, consumables, enchants, attendance, all scored. A raider-rank system that promised, in writing, that showing up mattered. And the loot log. Its very first row, 25 March 2020, credits Galidia with Bracelets of Wrath from Blackwing Lair. Galidia, a quiet warrior from the Nittedal circle, is in the ledger's opening line the same way he was everywhere else, without ever needing the credit for it. Four days later Artimis made the exercise official with a plea that every class leader record every drop, and everything the loot pages on this site know now descends from that one row. The spreadsheets were the guild's constitution, and like all constitutions they created their own politics. The paladin class-leader chair itself changed hands twice in ten weeks. Thepaladin took it from Randyfist on 5 March ("Its your job now :p") after Randyfist stepped away burned out, before the Nightfall planned for him could even be crafted. Weeks later, days after finally winning the argument over the Eye of Sulfuras, Thepaladin lost his own account to a hacker. Someone logged into his character and even gquit the guild with it, while Luivi and Shakeyhealer watched it happen and could only warn him afterward. Exclus, who split his raids between his mage and a holy paladin alt, Exclusafk, covered the class-leader gap for a week, then declined the permanent seat because he wasn't playing enough. The two characters logged nearly a hundred raids between them over the guild's life. Randyfist accepted the chair back on 22 April.

The raider-rank promise kept sharpening through the same weeks. Raiders reserved a place by signing up twenty-four hours ahead of a raid and risked demotion if their attendance fell below sixty percent over the last ten raids. Promotion from member to raider originally needed ten logged raids and an open class slot, a bar Artimis lowered to six on 15 March once it was clearly filtering out ordinary people rather than filtering in effort. Only class leaders could bench a player, and anyone who signed up, showed up prepared, and was then officially benched still kept the attendance credit. None of it was hardcore in spirit. It was forty people needing a repeatable answer to the same weekly argument: who plays, and who decides.

The Eye debate brought another friend from home into the record. By 27 March Panadin had gathered all fifty Arcanite Bars for his own Sulfuron Hammer, which under the guild's existing rule meant he could roll on the Eye too. Thepaladin, first in the queue for months, did not hide how little he liked sharing it:

That doesnt make me happy... Retadin mainspec tho and was first and so on.

— Thepaladin, 22 March 2020

He asked the officers directly whether he was being "very selfish" to resent it, or whether there was actually a rule to be made, then closed with the line that made the whole argument survivable: "But I will understand any decision we make." Artimis and Ragie held the original order. Panadin kept the second Eye place because Thepaladin was raiding more, not because his own hammer had arrived later. Panadin accepted it and kept showing up, at raids and at the Among Us evenings he organized for the group outside them.

A few days earlier, on 18 March, Shakeyhealer, the priest leader Dejvid had appointed before Artimis ever took over, won the roll for the first Rejuvenating Gem. Fnats had spent two months trying to get the gem's priority settled, and eight days waiting for an answer about the healer-officer role he had applied for on 10 March, with a cooldown-assignment sheet built from his own prior mythic raiding. Artimis offered him the role that same evening. It was a few hours too late. Fnats paused the game instead, on good terms and out of patience, with a sentence every guild leader knows: "as guild leader, you unfortunately stand in the crossfire."

The Gem was wanted by every healing class and stayed useful across several phases. Sleep's proposed answer was to let one healer from each class qualify for the roll. It sounded fair until the first one dropped and every unresolved question arrived with it. Who qualified, and who decided? Could months of work create a claim the rules could not express? Fnats had helped design priorities for items like this and never received one. His exams and accumulated frustration, not the single roll, were why he stepped away. Still, the timing made the lesson difficult to miss. A transparent process could distribute an item without resolving what people believed they deserved. Fnats later returned. His final local raid log is 30 August, not the night he first stepped back.

The spreadsheet could say no even to a legendary. On 12 March Thepaladin admitted he had secretly bought Nightfall after the officers rejected funding a second axe. Three days later Sleep tested it in Molten Core and measured the debuff uptime. His damage was then compared with a regular melee spot. Artimis's verdict was plain: "Does not seem worth it." Thepaladin kept the axe. The raid kept the numbers.

The people inside the machine

Artimis held the Guild Master title, but Sleep never ran on one person. It worked because responsibility spread outward. Luivi was tracking druid loot before Artimis became Guild Master. Pnats's path into the healing-officer chair took about a month. Thepaladin nominated him on 15 March ("maybe Pnats would be good"). Pnats declined for the moment on the 19th, after a confused Vaelastrasz pull, then applied himself on the 28th once passive coordination started costing wipes. His first raid running the role, on 1 April, meant reviewing the Vaelastrasz, Firemaw and Nefarian assignments the morning after. By the 2nd he had already drafted a cross-class healer-loot list for the next tier. Zados brought in Warcraft Logs, then helped build the hunter gear packages that kept every hunter from waiting for the same drop. The systems belonged to the people willing to maintain them.

Supplier arrived because Sleep needed healers and Artimis asked a close friend for help. The exact invitation is lost, but the speed of what followed is not. Artimis linked him a priest trinket on 22 February. By 8 March he had respecced Holy, completed his Molten Core attunement, signed for his first Sleep raid, and been promoted to member. On the 23rd he received The Eye of Divinity and completed the Benediction event on his first attempt. Two days later he made raider. Within days of joining he was diagnosing weak dispels and overhealing, recommending new addons, proposing clearer assignments. He had not so much joined the healing team as begun repairing it.

Some of the Nittedal contribution stayed quiet by nature. Glorya kept healing raids all spring without much fuss, the kind of steady presence the logs remember better than the drama ever does. Neptun, Ragie's girlfriend, first appears in the logs on 29 March and stayed into the Naxxramas core. She was another member of the Nittedal circle whose place in the story becomes clearest near the end. Neither she nor Galidia has a documented recruitment announcement, so the logs mark their presence rather than the date they joined.

By June Supplier was the priest leader, publishing loot priorities before raids and offering to bench himself if all five priests showed up. Divinestorm had risen the same way, months before anyone considered him for Guild Master. In April he noticed that mage coordination depended on three people, but rather than campaign around the sitting class leader, Procto, he waited. A nine-minute call with Artimis on 26 April made the promotion official, and only then did he build the mage roster, attendance and loot document, fix the Mageblade priorities, and delegate to Jumanzy whenever work travel took him offline. In Sleep, leadership was usually an appointment made after someone had already started doing the work.

By early summer the machine was humming and its operator was tired. In July, Zados succeeded Artimis as hunter leader in the same low-key way almost everyone else had been promoted, a formal title for a job he had effectively been sharing since the Warcraft Logs message that started this whole record. Sleep was clearing everything Classic had opened, recruiting faster than it lost people, and quietly wearing out the handful of people who ran it. The next chapter of this story is what happened when Artimis tried to give it away, twice, and what the guild became instead.

The story of Sleep · Chapter two

Dusk

A guild master tries to give his guild away, a rival guild privately plans to absorb it, and the merged creature proves everyone wrong — then nearly tears itself apart anyway. Summer and autumn, 2020.

Giving it away

By spring 2020 Artimis had learned what every volunteer institution eventually teaches its founder-figure: the job is permanent unless you make it stop. His first attempt was Kodf, the rogue leader whose loot system ran so cleanly it became proverbial — offered the guild outright, he declined, and on 9 July followed his own road elsewhere, to a guild called Conviction. Even from there he kept coaching his old guild through the Ninjas merger, world-buff routes and, weeks later, Viscidus preparation. The second attempt changed history: in July, Sleep offered the guild-master seat to Luivi, the druid leader. Between hospital shifts and the rest of his real life he didn't have time for it either — but instead of just saying no, he asked his contacts in a guild called Ninjas whether they'd consider merging.

Kodf's departure exposed how much of Sleep's stability had lived in one class leader. The senior rogues declined the vacancy, so a newcomer named Twx took it after three raids and replaced seniority with performance loot: parses, world buffs, spec and consumables would now decide who got gear, not who had shown up longest. Within two weeks he had awarded himself two tier pieces, and Tsakimo — one of the class's steadiest raiders — had quit rather than chase the new terms. Ntekapaz brought the complaints to Artimis, who checked Twx's performance claims against the raid logs and found they didn't hold up; his private wish that week was simply for "some rogue that can do Kodf's system." Twx, separately chasing a Thunderfury of his own, at one point weighed a five-thousand-gold pug Binding purchase and later claimed to already own one, a claim the officers never verified. He was gone before Dusk formed. The merger did not begin with two stable guilds; Sleep was already trying to replace one of the people who made its rules work.

Ninjas said yes in public and something else in private. Their officer chat from those weeks — preserved in full — is a study in swagger: "to call it a merger is a meme, since we don't need them at all. Better to call it absorbing their guild." They were not even short of options — a rival suitor called BABOONS had floated its own merger a fortnight earlier, which made picking Sleep feel, from their side, like a favour. Bandito, after watching a Sleep raid to scout the merchandise, reported back: "I highly doubt they've got the quality to clear AQ." Even so, one of their own noticed the asymmetry running the other way: Pawsz would later admit he was "a bit surprised" the merger imported stricter raider-tier rules from the side everyone assumed was weaker. The Ninjas officers took a formal vote by emoji on 15 August. The proposal carried.

On the Ninjas side the idea reached back further than the emoji vote: Snjór told his own officers on 12 July that Sleep's approach amounted to a guild "open to merge and have 2 raid groups," and Pawsz made the case just as plainly — "sleep needs 3 garr bindings, we need 2 geddons," meaning more Bindings of the Windseeker toward both sides' unfinished Thunderfury. The bargain kept that shape once the two AQ teams were running: Snjór, asked that autumn to off-tank the Twin Emperors undergeared, complained he now had to "pay for double consumables… or possibly [tell] wednesday raid team soz" — the same fight Glory had already lost on the Sleep side, just in Ninjas' colours. Two guilds sharing a calendar solved everyone's loot math; nobody's gear budget came free.

The handover

Between those two dates, Artimis staged the most graceful exit the story contains. Divinestorm was not an instant yes either: weeks earlier he had hesitated over his own work commitments and offered only to take loot duties off Artimis's plate, before agreeing, after Blackwing Lair on 2 August, that he was ready for the rest of the job. On the evening of 2 August 2020, Nefarian died twice. Artimis remembers telling the raid after the kill that he was going to be a father and that Divinestorm would be the new Guild Master. The spoken announcement has not been recovered, but the guild-system handoff, the congratulations and Artimis's public farewell all survive. Memory and record meet on the same evening: a planned abdication, mid-raid, after a boss kill.

The public record is warmer than the private one. In the same raid, in voice, Artimis told the group he was going to be a father; screenshots from that minute catch Pnats whispering "GRATS!" and "Didnt know," while Jumanzy asked, in character, what class the baby would be. Pnats's written message needled Artimis not to "do a Dejvid" — quit right after handing over the guild, a callback only the founding era would catch. Artimis did the opposite: he kept raiding and kept teaching Divinestorm the attendance and loot spreadsheets for weeks afterward. Privately, the same evening, he was less settled. Telling a departing guildmate that the merger decision now belonged to the officers who wanted it and was "out of my hands," he admitted he wouldn't have chosen it himself; the reply he got back was blunt with experience — mergers, in that player's telling, usually cost a guild its people. Twelve days later, Sleep became Dusk.

A new name

Artimis had titled the negotiation itself "Project Libra," and on 3 August members were told plainly what it meant: not one guild swallowing the other, but an entirely new guild built around two Ahn'Qiraj teams, with everyone's standing carried over intact. Three days later members were asked, formally, whether to simply join Ninjas or build the new guild instead. The merged guild needed a name neither side owned, so they polled one into existence: Dusk. It was a new guild on paper — both rosters walked into it. The poll made the principle visible: neither Sleep nor Ninjas would absorb the other. When Crok objected during the poll that any name built on "ninja" implied stealing loot, Divinestorm gave the answer he gave everyone: Dusk was a subtle nod to both sides, "the darker stage of twilight," in Artimis's own definition, not a winner's flag.

The fairness had to be engineered, not just declared. Divinestorm privately recommended that Sleep stand up the actual in-game guild while Ninjas migrated its own Discord server, a division of labor meant to keep either side from looking like it had taken control. The seam still showed that first week: on the new guild's opening AQ40 night, Exclusafk couldn't even sign up, because he had no role yet on the Dusk Discord, while the old Sleep server still ran a parallel signup sheet. Divinestorm's own summary of his time as Sleep's guild master, offered the week Dusk went live, was drier than anyone's: he had been GM of Sleep briefly, he said, and would be GM of Dusk even more briefly.

The doubters answered

Dusk had something to prove immediately because the Ninjas doubts came with a deadline: Ahn'Qiraj. On 18 August, its first raid night, four bosses died without a wipe before the Twin Emperors stopped the advance. Two nights later the Twins fell on the third pull. C'Thun survived four attempts; one reached seven percent health.

When the Thursday raid — built mostly around the old Ninjas roster — returned on 23 August, C'Thun died on the first pull. Ouro followed the same way. Five days after Dusk's first raid, the boss that had defined the tier was dead, for one of Dusk's two teams. Only Viscidus held out. Killing that poison elemental required a volunteer supply chain: roughly eight hundred and forty venom sacs, farmed from Lower Blackrock Spire by night-crew regulars like Crok, with advice relayed from Kodf even though he'd left the guild a month earlier, while Pawsz tracked consumption per attempt so the effort wouldn't be wasted twice. On 3 September, after two more wipes, Viscidus dissolved on the third attempt. The Thursday team was 9/9 sixteen days after Dusk's first raid — a number good enough that nobody advertising it mentioned the other team.

That other team met almost nobody's attention, which was rather the point of the whole arrangement. The Wednesday raid, built around Artimis's own group, ran the same instance on its own separate nights: an opening on 19 August, then C'Thun on 26 August, four days behind the Thursday kill. Tsakimo, who had quit Sleep in July over Twx's loot system, came back for that first Wednesday night and asked for spreadsheet access before he'd finished resettling into the guild. The Wednesday team didn't reach its own 9/9 until 9 September, a full week after the Thursday side, once Ouro and Viscidus both fell the same evening. Two AQ teams, two separate finish lines, one guild advertising a single triumphant number — the arrangement sold as fairness in August was already, by September, running as two guilds sharing a Discord server and not much else. The doubters had their answer anyway, and to their credit, they continued as if they had never doubted.

One crisis a fortnight

What AQ couldn't do to Dusk, Dusk nearly did to itself. The autumn officer channel reads like a guild getting tested every two weeks and barely passing. Late August: the Nayla case. What began as an apparent ninja pull in AQ20 was described by Divinestorm and Rockafella, after they spoke with him directly, as a hearthstone-while-teleported misunderstanding; the surviving evidence cannot independently establish whether that account fully cleared him. The case also included an accusation that he had been overly flirtatious toward two other members and rage-quit the run. It still reopened older friction, and the officers split along predictable lines: Missdotty wanted him gone ("his behavior is very toxic"), Luivi offered a "third or fourth chance," and Orish argued the opposite case on principle — "any man willing to take a punishment on the chin... has loyalty you can't buy." The verdict split the difference: "one final chance with punishment — call it a fresh start for Dusk." The telephone game continued past the verdict: on 9 September Nayla told Tsakimo he'd lost his tanking spot and would reroll rogue, which Luivi had never said and Nayla himself later denied saying. "It's Nayla," Divinestorm shrugged, "I doubt the guy remembers what he said half the time" — a line that closed the incident without resolving whether anyone had actually told Nayla anything at all.

Two older Sleep names disappear from the raid record before the loudest crisis arrived. Pricelez's last guild raid was 2 September, after ninety-eight logged appearances; Conoflow's was 13 September, after ninety-five. Neither left a surviving goodbye. Their dates belong to the attrition story, but the archive cannot say whether the coming disputes caused either departure.

17 September: Akikiie, a tank and the man who had built the Ninjas' Discord server, pulled at the AQ entrance with nobody ready. The world buffs evaporated. Ulfhild, the guild's best hunter and a man who played for the parse, quit the raid on the spot. The rogues nearly walked with him; Antzi, their class leader, admitted afterwards that he'd talked them into staying with arguments he didn't believe. The fight that followed ran three days and came down to one question: is this a hardcore guild or a home? Divinestorm staked out the guild's identity plainly — "we are not a hardcore guild… what example does it set if nothing happens?" — while Pawsz worried the pull had "felt close to raid sabotage." When Òllz told Divinestorm to "fuck off" rather than back down from defending Ulfhild, Artimis stepped in with the only reprimand of its kind in the whole archive: "Please moderate your language, Òllz. We don't speak to each other like that." Nobody settled the underlying question. Akikiie apologised, wondered out loud if he even enjoyed the game anymore, and left the guild that week. Snjór's private postmortem was more forgiving than the public argument had been: Akikiie "left cause of his immaturity," he said, "but I don't want to drag him through the mud." Two days later, almost as if to prove the drama and the progression ran on separate tracks, the raid killed Ossirian and closed out Ruins of Ahn'Qiraj without him.

A week later, Orish quit live in the officer channel. He was the Thursday raid's healing brain — a player who had leveled three separate warlocks purely so other people's world buffs would have someone to summon them — and the leadership's fiercest internal critic. He collided with Divinestorm over a chaotic split Sunday run, and his last message was "Leadership's going to run this guild into the ground, I'm out before it gets much worse." That made two ex-Ninjas officers gone in seven days. By afternoon Ferrua was running healer loot with zero notice — "Does this mean I decide healer loot today?" — and doing it well enough that nobody complained.

The response, within the hour, was structural, and Rockafella supplied the argument that decided it: "class leaders think they are officers... if the class leaders could shut their mouth and run their class and let the officers manage the guild decisions, we wouldn't have this shit." Class leaders lost their seats in the inner room, and the officer chat split in two.

The split reopened an older argument: how much of any of this the ordinary membership should ever see. Pawsz floated radical transparency — "cropping the last 3 days of officer chat and posting them on a pastebin would achieve the same at max transparency" — but Pnats worried about handling "another Nayla situation if we allow the chat to be public," and Zados predicted only that "we would just have people trying to influence our decisions." The same week showed how little the merger had actually merged: Luivi still drew the line at "ex ninjas members… ex sleep raid is fine," and Pnats admitted the plainer truth — "our members think its down to us (Sleep) atm because two of you (Ninjas) left."

The new arrangement didn't end the friction, it just moved it downstairs. Three days later, someone floated giving the Band of Accuria to Jangera as a reward for reaching Rank 14 in the honor system; Antzi detonated at the suggestion, told Rockafella to "stick to ur lock," and then felt accused of stupidity by an officer in the same exchange. Supplier talked it back down. Snjór's verdict on the whole culture doubled as a farewell to his own guild's founding spirit: "I'm seeing three different chat discussions about some item that's going to increase their DPS by 0.0000001%… the mentality we set in Ninjas was always entertainment before some purple pixels." And on the 30th of September the autumn delivered its finale. The surviving accounts agree on the opening act: Yeat kicked Snjór. Henne said someone then reinvited Snjór and that he, or his alt, used the restored rank to kick Henne. Snjór's account four days later agreed that Yeat had kicked him but disputed the surrounding blame. The archive does not establish who authorized the reinvite or the second kick, nor why each person in the protest wave left.

The consequences are clearer than the cause. Yeat transferred Guild Master to Missdotty and left. By the next morning, thirty-two people were signed for the coming raid and eight of those names belonged to people who had left. Divinestorm, mediating from a work trip, handled member communication while Missdotty restricted kick access. Henne told others that same day she meant to follow Katdrovrsn, Mikadrovrsn and Òllz out of the guild; four days later Snjór reported that she and Òllz were staying after all. Henne's own condition for peace was the tersest in the archive: "Keep Snjór away from me and he can take the drama with him. And I'm good." The reform that outlived the mess became guild doctrine, written by Artimis in the class-leader channel: if you don't have time to explain, you don't have time to kick people.

The quiet erosion

Under the fireworks, something slower was happening: the old Sleep establishment was losing the building. In the September admin reshuffle, the founders' names — Artimis, Zados, Celed — were flagged off the server-admin list, and when Divinestorm deleted a channel Zados liked, his reply is the driest sentence Dusk produced: "I guess I'm not an officer anymore, so whatever." Divinestorm, meanwhile, had become the guild's center of gravity. He mediated the crises, wrote the loot doctrine (attendance, world buffs, consumes, consistency), and personally ran the idol economy, item by item. He also absorbed abuse that would have broken a pettier leader. The loot doctrine even got a quiet vindication in those same weeks: after a Rejuvenating Gem awarded to an alt gave Artimis and Zados one more spark for the September argument, Luivi conceded on 1 October that, in hindsight, Ferrua had been right to withhold a Rejuvenating Gem from Mikadrovrsn in the first place. By November the transformation was complete in all but ceremony: Dusk was Divinestorm's guild, run well, by a mage the Sleep founders had recruited eight months earlier. Celed, one of those flagged-off founders, didn't outlast the season either: his own last guild raid fell on 24 September, the same day as the wider ex-Ninjas exodus.

Where the departed went afterward is only partly recoverable. Warcraft Logs' historical guild feeds confirm that Celed, Snjór, Seax and Szata resurfaced in a guild called Agile, and that Banamahs joined Decimation — Luivi called him "another Decimation recruit" within minutes of the announcement. Antzi insisted on 1 October that he hadn't left at all, only paused; his later reports, like Pawsz's, Henne's, Ulfhild's, Yeat's and Orish's, continue in the logs but never match a guild's own historical feed closely enough to name a destination. Yeat, who gave up the guild-master rank the same day he left, has no destination in the record either, and Orish's first outside report is more than three weeks after he quit live in the channel. For most of the people this chapter loses, the record can prove they kept raiding somewhere. It cannot always prove where.

One more departure closes the way Celed, Snjór, Seax and Szata did. Jangera, gone chasing bigger AQ40 GDKP gold that October, resurfaces thirty-one times against Melon's own historical guild feed once that first circuit ends — a destination the record can confirm, not merely infer. For the longer list from that same autumn — Pawsz, Henne, Ulfhild, Antzi, Yeat, Orish, Tsakimo — the identical cross-check comes back empty: not proof they stopped playing, only that wherever they landed left no trace to catch.

The Nittedal circle was thinning too, but not through one shared goodbye. Panadin's last local raid is 12 July. Fnats last appears on 30 August, Exclus on 20 September, and Galidia and Zhèna on 7 October. None of those dates comes with a preserved departure message, so they are boundaries in the logs, not explanations — unlike Akikiie, Orish or Yeat, none of them argued their way out; the guild simply stopped seeing them on the signup sheet. Deliz remained the exception: the one friend whose exit survives in his own words.

Nayla's ending had a fuller private context than the November accusation alone suggests. On 18 November, the date of his last tracked raid, he told officers he had felt disliked ever since an earlier incident pulling a dragon into Stormwind, that other tanks received raid assist he didn't, and that he had experienced "tyranny, disrespect" building for months rather than one incident. The officers' reply cited loot and assist records for Ragie, Glory and Boxya to argue the treatment had been equal; Artimis asked directly why Nayla was leaving and received no answer. Neither account is a verdict — the archive preserves the grievance and the rebuttal, not which one was right. Two days later Divinestorm told the officer channel that Nayla had joined a guild called Inheritance.

November gave the era its two closing images. On the 7th, Glory's second Binding dropped and the guild announced a completed Thunderfury the same evening: the loyalist's crowning, three weeks before his heartbreak. Nayla's own ending landed eleven days later. On the 25th, already in his new guild, he sent an image and accused Dusk's leader of a "last desperate act of evilness." Artimis later identified the reference as the earlier AQ20 disruption case. The officers had reconstructed that incident as a hearthstone-while-teleported misunderstanding; whether that fully cleared Nayla or was simply the gentlest available explanation remains unresolved. Behind both endings, quietly, stood Naxxramas — frost-resistance spreadsheets filling in, a contingency tank being groomed, and a raid that had no idea it had fourteen days of existence left. That story is chapter three.

The story of Sleep · Chapter three

Fourteen Days in December

How a friendly guild cleared the last raid of Classic — and stopped existing to do it. December 2020, reconstructed from the raid logs, the officer channel, and private messages from several of the people involved.

The wall

On the first Sunday of December 2020, Dusk walked into Naxxramas alone and killed three bosses — Anub'Rekhan, Grand Widow Faerlina and Noth the Plaguebringer. By any earlier standard of the guild it was a fine night — Artimis clipped the Faerlina kill, which would turn out to be the last recording of Dusk raiding as itself. But Naxxramas was built to punish exactly what Dusk was: a community first and a roster second. Divinestorm's review of the run, in the officer channel, was two words: "utter shit".

Grand Widow Faerlina — 6 December

Dusk did not know it yet, but the same week held a preview of where this was going. Unconnected to Dusk's raid, Ascension's own thirty-nine-player team killed five bosses that same evening in a separate Naxxramas raid — Anub'Rekhan, Faerlina, Maexxna, Noth and Heigan the Unclean, a fight Dusk's own raid never reached that night. The next night Ascension's raid added five more: Loatheb, Patchwerk, Grobbulus, Gluth and Thaddius. Ten distinct bosses in two nights, against Dusk's three — the gap the merger would soon close.

The next evening the floor gave way. Edomedo — the quiet warrior Fizzi had flagged a month earlier as the contingency plan ("if the situation with Boxya happens again or worse we have a potential tank lined up at least") — told Divinestorm he was quitting the game. Four Horsemen needs four tanks working in parallel, not four turns of a well-drilled strategy; Dusk had two proven ones in Ragie and Fizzi, and had spent November building Edomedo into the third. Even fully staffed, the thirty-six raiders Dusk fielded on the 6th were four short of the forty Naxxramas assumed. Divinestorm looked at the roster that night and reached the conclusion that ended an era: there were no tanks for the Four Horsemen, and no way to make any inside the guild.

The pivot

The tone of the turn survives in Divinestorm's own dark joke on the 8th, tossed off in the same message as the deal itself: he had collected Sleep and Dusk, he said, and would soon have Ascension too. It read as gallows humor from a man who had called the previous raid "utter shit" two days earlier and was now brokering the guild's third identity in under five months.

What happened next took about a day. On December 8th Divinestorm laid out the case in officer chat and reported the solution he had already found: Ascension — a guild the archive had barely met, except as a world-chat troll to be ignored back in September — was "prepared to bench down to 20 people on Wednesday and give us 20 slots." Their officers had names now, Devou the warrior and Hedara the druid, and they were candid about the endgame: they would like to stay Ascension, and absorb Dusk. Divinestorm reported the need ran both ways, not as charity: their GM, he said, had told him bluntly "we need this more than you," rather than see Ascension dissolve into "random players in goons etc." Not everyone was charmed — Zados registered "a pretty bad impression of ascension players so far" — but nobody had a second plan.

No Ascension negotiation transcript survives. Their position reaches this archive through Divinestorm's report of the call and through what their officers did next. Devou controlled the joint composition and, when his own warrior could not attend on 9 December, offered Dusk a third hunter place. The raid log is the independent boundary: forty-one names passed through a forty-player team built from twenty returning Dusk raiders and twenty Ascension players, with one substitution. It proves the arrangement, not every claim made while negotiating it.

Building that template still meant choosing who among each guild's own regulars made the cut, and Ascension controlled the finer print. Fizzi did the calculation that mattered most for his own class — "atm Ascension only has 2 hunters," he told the officers — which meant Dusk's three hunters were negotiating for room against a partner that barely needed the position at all. Devou, running composition from the Ascension side, stayed just as firm when a fourth hunter spot was floated later in the month: two was the number, and the answer never moved. The template treated both twenty-name rosters as fixed pools to be defended, not one combined list to be freely optimized, which is exactly why the cuts kept landing on the same handful of classes.

Twenty spots meant twenty benches, and the cut fell hardest on the classes Ascension already had. The hunters got the worst arithmetic: Ascension wanted two hunter spots in a forty-man raid; Dusk had three hunters who had carried the guild for a year. "I'd feel like a dick picking who dies," Zados said while the math was still being drawn, "since they aren't that far apart, all bringing the required consumables, trying their best." Zados, handed the impossible job of choosing, negotiated it into a three-week rotation between himself, Nereb and Artimis — and Artimis, whose first child was due on the 28th, took the first bench to line his absence up with the hospital.

The document that would decide who sat had Artimis's own fingerprints on it. That same morning he built the shared spreadsheet the officers used to sort the raid ("Help me see how this will look here"), argued the merger was worth the risk even if the first joint run went roughly, and told the room where to draw the line if a class leader couldn't decide: "If a class leader find it hard who to bench then look at attendance at least." He wrote the rule six days before it was used against him. He also kept proposing patches rather than just accepting the math: for the heaviest healing checks, he suggested, he and Zados could swap onto their paladin and priest alts and free two ranged-DPS slots for extra healers. Nothing came of it, but it captured his instinct through the whole crisis — solve the arithmetic before it solves you.

Pnats accepted the arithmetic and removed himself from it. After reading the proposal he wrote that Dusk's current team would most likely never survive to clear Naxxramas and called the merger "100% the only way to go." Then he stepped out, unable to promise the weekly consumable farm while playing Shadowlands too. Supplier had made the same case hours earlier, and more bluntly: "If you don't put in the effort or have the skills to clear Naxx, there is no reason you should be carried through Naxx" — and offered his own name first: "I am also happy to self-bench if needed." The merger was not only leaders cutting other people; some of the people who agreed with it chose themselves for the bench.

The blitz

It worked. On December 9th the first joint raid killed six new bosses in one evening — Patchwerk, Grobbulus, Gluth, Thaddius, Heigan, Razuvious — and the kill videos carried both guild names in their titles. Artimis even got to be there: Devou's warrior couldn't make it, and the 21st spot passed to him. "Don't thank me," Divinestorm told him afterwards. "Thank Devou for understanding how much we wanted a 3rd hunter spot."

Noth the Plaguebringer — 9 December Patchwerk — 9 December Thaddius — 9 December Instructor Razuvious — 9 December Heigan the Unclean — hunter view Instructor Razuvious — priest tank view

But the same speed that vindicated the merge hardened its logic. "Comp plus parse," as Fizzi put it — with limited spots you bring the players who perform. His own place in that first raid made the point better than any policy memo: three weeks earlier Fizzi and Glory had tanked C'Thun side by side, and only one of them survived the cut into Naxxramas. Attendance, the currency the guild had minted for a year, quietly stopped being legal tender. On the 12th, Artimis found out Divinestorm wouldn't spare him Frozen Runes for frost-resistance gear, and heard he'd been passed over for players with four and nine raids to their names against his hundred and twenty. His fury went to Zados in private, and Zados, who agreed with him, could only answer: "They have a hard-on for limiting hunter spots. I'll do what I can."

The Sunday session that produced the fury was the same one that exposed the tank math: twelve wipes on the Horsemen in that Sunday's raid, the best attempt stalling at forty-four percent, while both Artimis and Glory watched from outside. That evening Pnats opened the officer channel with the complaint that mattered: leaving Artimis out felt "a bit... harsh," given "the value the dude has brought to Sleep & Dusk" — benched, in Pnats's words, "for a low attendance Warrior." Divinestorm's answer pointed past the name everyone assumed: "he's not benching for Maniaz, he's benching for Tansus, or Barin, or Biterx. That 21st spot wasn't ours to begin with." Supplier's reply was the plainest sentence in the whole dispute: "We still choose our 20 guys in the end."

At eight minutes past midnight on December 14th, Zados delivered a message that would echo: Divinestorm said he'll bench you for Wednesday if you don't come tomorrow. Asked whether he agreed, Zados wrote: "I don't agree, no. But he does have the final say. Unfortunately."

The letter

Glory arrived at this week already holding the guild's proof of concept. Five weeks earlier his second Binding of the Windseeker had dropped and the guild had forged him a completed Thunderfury — the raider-rank promise made physical: show up, do the unglamorous work, and the guild builds you the most famous weapon in the game. He had studied Ascension's own Four Horsemen kill video frame by frame and planned a respec to deep Protection for the survivability the fight demanded. None of it bought him a pull.

The Four Horsemen — Ascension reference kill

Artimis's letter was not his first move. At 19:57 the night before, he had already asked Glorya directly — noting her own perfect attendance — to simply not show up for Wednesday's raid, naming Pnats and Supplier as already committed and hoping Ragie and Luivi would follow. Ragie's answer became the caution that shaped everything after it: members shouldn't be displaced by newcomers, he agreed, but organising behind Divinestorm's back was the wrong tool, and two guilds were still sharing one roster. Past midnight, when word of Divinestorm's benching threat reached him secondhand, Artimis forwarded the screenshot to Glorya with his own verdict attached, in Norwegian: "Trussler er ikke bra" — threats aren't good. The morning's five-point letter gave that same anger a form that could finally be argued instead of whispered.

Artimis spent the morning of the 14th writing. The letter made five demands: raiders before members, a spot held for the whole lockout, a place at least every other week for raider rank, bench decisions made collectively, and the optional Monday raid staying truly optional. It ended with a call for the members of Dusk to strike on Wednesday the 16th. He sent it to Shakeyhealer at 11:02 and to Glory at 11:20 — the tank who had been benched through every Four Horsemen wipe, and who had been told that afternoon he finally had a spot.

Only go on strike if your demands dont get a fair hearing. Risking losing far more than you gain. Of course i agree, more or less.

— Glory, 14 December 2020

The strike never fired, and the archive can now say exactly why. In the officers' channel that same morning, Divinestorm opened his own reckoning: an admission that he had asked Maniaz to cover Sunday "without remembering we had 21 people for the first run," and the hunter math sitting underneath the whole argument — "Ascension want a total of 2 hunter spots in the full raid. We want 4." Then he went further than policy required: "I'll never feel really good about this unless we have Artimis on the team… I can give up my spot for him."

Pnats backed Artimis by name in the same channel — "Artimis is a special case, so is Zados and Nereb… one of the near-founders of the Guild" — in the same message where he reached for Glory as the counter-example, an aside he half-apologised for even as he made it. Minutes later, in a verdict never delivered to Glory's face, Pnats told the other officers plainly: "Glory has had a year and a half trial, and not improved"; "he isn't good enough." Zados made the same case from a different angle, naming the replacement who hadn't earned his spot: "We brought Juud instead of someone like Artimis, yet he didnt do shit as a tank." Supplier pulled the argument back to principle: "this is not about Artimis per se. It's the general respect of our raiders, how they are treated in accordance with the rules we set." Luivi, watching from the sidelines, made the simplest argument of all: the players who had started this journey together should be the ones who finished it.

Pnats, mediating from inside the same channel, asked the harder question before he backed anyone at all: had Artimis actually spoken to Divinestorm directly before agreeing to any of this? He supported raider-over-member priority, attendance and whole-lockout places, but rejected automatic rotation for players he judged unable to execute Naxxramas progression, and said plainly that years of avoiding hard rank decisions had finally caught up with the guild.

Not everyone wanted compromise. The day before, Randyfist had floated an even blunter version — let Ascension and a rival guild merge instead, and Dusk could "take their good players after they implode" — before arguing the same case again in the officer channel: "poach the benched Ascension raiders, cancel the merge, Ascension implodes, we get half their shit — just like a good ol' divorce." Nobody took the advice. That same night, he was the one holding the raid's mythic reward.

In a separate private thread that same day, Pnats begged Artimis to bring the five points into the open: "You have many points. Which everyone should see." Artimis refused, with the sentence that explains the whole December record: "If I involve myself, it will only become about me." Shakeyhealer worked the back channel instead, taking the points to Divinestorm softly — and privately confessed he might not make the move to Ascension at all. By late afternoon Divinestorm was chasing Artimis directly: "I know you've been keeping up with the CL chat. I know your points. Let's just do this tonight and talk." At 18:34, they talked.

That instinct — rules over favors — hardened into a formal proposal by afternoon. Supplier turned the private anger into a policy intervention. He had initially agreed to join a protest, then asked for concrete demands and warned against language that could destroy the guild. He carried a revised version to Divinestorm: rank priority subject to role minimums, whole-lockout places, rotation for retained Raiders, collective bench decisions, and a genuinely optional Monday. He even offered Artimis his own priest place on Lege. Artimis declined; the point was a rule others could rely on, not a private exception for himself.

And then December did the most December thing in the whole story. Artimis, conciliatory after the call, went out for his evening walk and offered to log on when he got back. The raid filled without him — and the Four Horsemen, the fight that had eaten twelve pulls on Sunday, died on the first attempt of the night at 19:42. Glory, whose promised spot had evaporated when a full forty showed up, listened to the winning briefing from the bench. Artimis watched Ragie's stream. "Happy to be part of a 13/15 team," he wrote. How much of that was sincere is anyone's guess. That night, Divinestorm invited him into the joint officer chat with Ascension. He accepted, and listened. When Hedara finally spoke with him directly, weeks later, he opened with a small proof of how tight the local scene already was: "You're from Norway, right? Your voice is familiar."

The first-pull chest held Corrupted Ashbringer. It went to Randyfist, and at 20:52 Artimis sent congratulations with a warning not to carry it into Light's Hope. At 21:34, in an offhand message to an outside raid leader, Artimis mentioned his guild had just killed the Four Horsemen — the detail that independently fixes the kill to that night, before the archive had time to turn it into a milestone. The raid kept going and found its harder truth soon after: Sapphiron ate eleven more wipes before the night ended, the best attempt stalling at sixty-five percent. The night of the strike letter ended with one of the game's most famous swords, and its next boss still unsolved.

The same 8 December planning message that had carved out the hunter rotation also named who the officers meant to protect elsewhere: Divinestorm told the room that he and Crok did not want to cut Neptun, Jumanzy or Electro, all three good enough to "perform very well" every week — he'd rather rotate his own spot away than lose theirs, he said, the same logic Pnats and Supplier would apply to themselves days later. Neptun was in the winning Four Horsemen roster. It is her last local raid log, on 14 December; the archive preserves no goodbye or reason for what followed.

The change

On the 16th, the day the strike was supposed to happen, the guild dissolved instead. The deciding exchange was almost comically casual:

Divinestorm's plan was to have everyone leave Dusk together as they zoned into the raid. Randyfist had forced the question that afternoon and answered it himself within minutes — unresolved things needled at him, he later explained, until he acted. He explained the order behind the impulse in the same breath he committed to it: "Gonna do it there now, i think its good to have some officers already in there before the migration" — the last operational instruction Dusk's leadership gave as Dusk, fittingly logistical to the end. Pnats, mid-migration, pinged his brother Ragie to take one last look at the channel before it emptied. At a quarter to midnight, Pnats wrote the era its epitaph:

At 23:31, while the migration was happening, Rockafella was still mailing materials to the benched Artimis and asking for two flasks. The guild could dissolve faster than its consumables pipeline. The officer channel itself barely outlived the decision: within a day it goes quiet, and every conversation from the night of the clear onward happened inside Ascension's own Discord — a record this archive never received.

Glory left the same evening, "better to go during the change": a hundred-and-twenty-raid tank who was never given a single Horsemen pull, saying the guild had been his home and that a spot every other week would have kept him. Within a week he was tanking the Four Horsemen for someone else.

The raid did not pause to grieve. That same evening, under its new name, the roster went back into Naxxramas and cleared eleven bosses without a single wipe (Anub'Rekhan through Instructor Razuvious), first pull after first pull. Whatever the migration had cost socially, it had not touched the raid's execution.

Fifteen of fifteen

The four days before the clear went into frost resistance, not politics. Supplier handed Artimis two guild-bank Frozen Runes on the 16th and 17th so he could finish the legs and ring the fight demanded. Zados coordinated an emergency Polar Tunic crafting run and combat-log coverage. Luivi spent the same window building resistance gear for Ilove, then logged in for the final night despite his father's car accident the day before — the raid briefly waited on him rather than start a druid short.

None of that scramble started from zero. Supplier had been mapping priest and druid set choices for Naxxramas boss by boss since 7 November, and had already leveled a profession alt purely to keep materials flowing for the guild's own tier pieces. The four days between the migration and the clear were a sprint finish, not the whole race.

Four days later it was done. Sapphiron died at 19:53 and Kel'Thuzad at 21:23 on December 20th. The last boss of vanilla World of Warcraft was dead fourteen days after Dusk first stepped into Naxxramas alone. Three wipes on the night; forty-three names in the log. Artimis streamed all of it, died early to a spell-batched ice block, and uploaded the video anyway — "I'll take credit for uploading it even if that's how I die." A few minutes past midnight Pnats sent him, verbatim: "Deiliiiig. We did it all!" — and Artimis answered "Bra jobba!!! Sykt kult at vi klarte det. Hadde ikke trodd det." Great job. Insanely cool that we made it. I didn't think we would.

Sapphiron — 20 December Kel'Thuzad — kill Kel'Thuzad — all three attempts and the kill

Pnats nearly missed the moment he had helped build toward: he dropped to two or three percent of his own health on the kill attempt, after tanking two stray Nerubian adds on the wipe before it. He was also the one who asked Artimis to upload Sapphiron and Kel'Thuzad in full, wipes included, so even the messy nights would survive as memory. Jumanzy, in the same round of post-raid screenshots, thanked Artimis simply for leading them through Sleep, Dusk and Ascension.

Five people from Nittedal stood in that forty-three-name log: Artimis, Glorya, Pnats, Ragie and Supplier. Ragie tanked the kill the same way he had tanked every progression fight before it, from the front, his brother healing behind him. For Artimis, Pnats and Ragie it was the last local guild raid. Supplier appears once more in early January, while Glorya's logs continue into February. It was not a single group departure. The friends who had arrived in layers faded from the raid archive the same way.

The epilogue happened quietly. Several of those who had stayed for the clear left the game within days, as they had said they would. Shakeyhealer had said as much around the migration, telling Artimis privately he might simply call it a day rather than follow the guild into Ascension at all, careful to add it wasn't a decision made in anger — and careful, days later, to check that Artimis was still inside the officer conversation and not quietly sidelined. He kept close to his word: by his own later account he stopped playing around the same time as Artimis and Supplier and never carried a character into the Burning Crusade. The next day, Divinestorm asked Artimis whether he wanted to keep raiding as Hunter or switch to Priest, noting that holiday availability and the same rotation agreement should open further spots either way — the rotation logic that had started the whole fight, now offered back as a small peace. Artimis's child arrived at the turn of the year ("Grattis som far!" reads the January message) and he kept raiding into the spring on whichever character was needed. And on the 11th of April 2021, in Glory's final local report, the guild's most-attended raider got the kill history had skipped him for: Glory's own Kel'Thuzad. He killed it alongside Divinestorm, Fizzi, Zados, Jumanzy, Amarah, Electro and Devou — the same names December had put together, four months and one boss later, finishing the job as a group.

The local archive has one more entry, a small Ascension raid on 14 April. The wider logs continue after that. On 25 April, Divinestorm appears in two Naxxramas reports with Hedara, Bearinpants, Ihide, Mikasalord and Taptum — the same December pairing of Dusk and Ascension names, still raiding together four months on.

On 12 May, Supplier posted one final inventory of Dusk: twenty-nine names, each paired with a tribute video.

A 15 May Naxxramas GDKP still carries Hadara, Hedara, Docthor, Saltylock and Taptum. They are traces of people continuing to play together, not proof that the old guild survived under another name.

The Discord server itself outlived every guild name ever attached to it. The channel a Ninjas officer built in April 2020, and Dusk inherited that August, never actually closed — it still exists today under yet another name, its rename history as unrecoverable as everything else the archive never received, and the account that once ran Sleep and Dusk still holds a seat inside it. Nobody planned that kind of survival. It was simply easier to keep a server running than to keep a guild alive.
